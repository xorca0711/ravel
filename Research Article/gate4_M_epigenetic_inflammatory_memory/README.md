# Gate 4M — Epigenetic & Inflammatory Memory

Gate 4 organizes readings around a biological interest. The first topic asks which epithelial changes persist after inflammation resolves, how they are retained, and whether they alter the response to a later injury or the growth of a tumour.

## Selected papers

| Paper | Biological setting | Central comparison |
|---|---|---|
| [Nagaraja et al. (2026) — Epigenetic memory of colitis promotes tumour growth](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10258-4) | Colonic epithelium | Persistence of a regenerative chromatin state after colitis and its association with subsequent tumour outgrowth. |
| [Naik et al. (2017) — Inflammatory memory sensitizes skin epithelial stem cells to tissue damage](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature24271) | Epidermal stem cells | Persistent accessibility after inflammation, followed by a functional comparison of wound re-epithelialization. |
| [Cowley et al. (2026) — Distinctive DNA sequence features define epigenetic longevity of inflammatory memory](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adz6830) | Skin epithelial chromatin | Sequence features that distinguish regions retaining accessibility from regions that resolve after inflammation. |

## Comparative interpretation

Nagaraja connects prior colitis to clonal growth after tumour initiation. Naik connects prior skin inflammation to faster wound repair. Cowley examines why some chromatin regions remain accessible longer than others. A sequence feature, a persistent cell state and an altered tissue response are distinct observations. Their connection is the organizing question for this topic.
